The most expensive pizza in history. So what exactly is bitcoin? Who invented it? Why is it worth so much? Chapter one. The problem with money. To understand bitcoin, we first need to talk about regular money. Think about the money in a bank account. It's not really sitting in a vault with your name on it. It's just a number, in a computer, owned by a bank. And for that number to work, we have to trust a lot of people. We trust the bank to keep track of it correctly. We trust the bank not to lose it. And we trust the government not to print so much new money that the money we have becomes worth less. Most of the time, that system works pretty well. But in {2008|two thousand eight}, the world got a scary reminder of what happens when that trust breaks. The global financial crisis hit. Huge banks had made reckless bets, and when those bets went bad, the whole system started to wobble. Governments stepped in and used trillions of dollars to rescue the banks. Meanwhile, millions of regular people lost their homes and their jobs. A lot of people were angry. And one person, somewhere in the world, decided to build an alternative.

Chapter two. The mysterious inventor. On October {thirty-first|thirty first}, {2008|two thousand eight}, right in the middle of the crisis, someone posted a short paper to an online mailing list for cryptography fans. It was only nine pages long. And its title was "Bitcoin: A {Peer-to-Peer|Peer to Peer} Electronic Cash System." The author's name was Satoshi Nakamoto. The idea was simple, but revolutionary. A kind of digital money that doesn't need a bank. No company in charge. No government in charge. Just people, all over the world, sending money directly to each other. On January third, {2009|two thousand nine}, Satoshi launched the bitcoin network. And hidden inside the very first block of data, Satoshi left a message. A newspaper headline from that day, from The Times in London. "Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks." It was like a timestamp. And a statement. This is why bitcoin exists. A few days later, Satoshi sent ten bitcoins to a computer scientist named Hal Finney. The first bitcoin transaction in history. For the next two years, Satoshi kept working on bitcoin with a small group of volunteers. Writing code. Answering questions. Fixing problems. And then, in {2011|twenty eleven}, Satoshi simply disappeared. A few final messages, and then nothing. Never heard from again. To this day, nobody knows who Satoshi Nakamoto really is. It could be one person. It could be a group. Many people have been suspected. Some have even claimed to be Satoshi. But none of them has ever proven it. And here's the wildest part. Satoshi is believed to have mined around one million bitcoins in the early days. Those coins have never moved. Not one. At today's prices, that would make Satoshi one of the richest people on Earth. Someone, somewhere, is sitting on a fortune worth many billions of dollars. And they have never touched it.

Chapter three. The magic notebook. So how does bitcoin actually work? Let's make it simple. Imagine a small village. Instead of using cash, everyone in the village keeps track of money in a big notebook. Every time someone pays someone else, it gets written down. Alice paid Bob five coins. Bob paid Carol two coins. Now, if only one person holds that notebook, you have to trust them completely. What if they cheat? What if they erase a page? What if they lose it? So the village has a better idea. Everyone gets their own copy of the notebook. Every time a payment happens, it's announced to the whole village, and everybody writes it down in their copy. Now, if one person tries to cheat and change their notebook, it won't match everyone else's. The village instantly knows it's fake. That notebook is basically what bitcoin is. It's called the blockchain. The blockchain is a giant public record of every bitcoin transaction ever made. And copies of it are stored on thousands of computers all around the world. No single person controls it. No single company can change it. And anyone can look at it. Payments get grouped together into pages, called blocks. Every block is linked to the block before it, like a chain. Hence, blockchain. And because each block is linked to the one before it, changing an old page would mean rewriting every page that came after it, on most of the computers in the world at once. Practically impossible.

Chapter four. Who writes in the notebook? Now here's the clever part. In our village, who actually gets to write the next page in the notebook? And why would anyone bother doing that work? This is where mining comes in. Bitcoin miners are computers that compete to add the next block to the blockchain. To win, they have to solve a really hard math puzzle. Well, it's less of a puzzle and more of a guessing game. The computers make trillions of guesses, over and over, until one of them gets lucky and finds the right answer. Whoever finds it first gets to add the next page to the notebook. And as a reward, they get brand new bitcoins. That's how new bitcoins are created. Not printed by a government. Earned by miners. Why make it so hard? Because it protects the system. If someone wanted to cheat and rewrite the notebook, they would need more computing power than most of the rest of the network combined. That would cost an unbelievable amount of money and electricity. So in bitcoin, honesty isn't just a good idea. It's the cheapest option. A new block is added roughly every ten minutes. And here's something smart. If more miners join and they start solving blocks too quickly, the puzzle automatically gets harder. If miners leave, it gets easier. It's like a thermostat that keeps bitcoin running at a steady pace.

Chapter five. Only {twenty-one|twenty one} million. Ever. Now we get to the reason many people love bitcoin. There will only ever be {twenty-one|twenty one} million bitcoins. Ever. That rule is written into bitcoin's code. Compare that to regular money. Governments can print more dollars, euros, or rupees whenever they decide to. And when there's more money around, each piece of it usually buys a little less. That's inflation. It's why a candy bar costs more today than it did when your parents were kids. Bitcoin works differently. The supply is fixed. And the new supply gets smaller over time. In the beginning, miners earned fifty bitcoins for every block. Then, about every four years, that reward gets cut in half. This is called the halving. Fifty became {twenty-five|twenty five} in {2012|twenty twelve}. Then twelve and a half in {2016|twenty sixteen}. Then six and a quarter in {2020|twenty twenty}. And in April {2024|twenty twenty four}, it dropped to just over three bitcoins per block. It keeps going like this, getting smaller and smaller, until roughly the year {2140|twenty one forty}, when the last tiny fraction of a bitcoin will be mined. That's why some people call bitcoin "digital gold." Gold is valuable partly because it's rare, and you can't just make more of it. Bitcoin was designed to be even more rare. And you don't need to buy a whole bitcoin. Each one can be split into one hundred million tiny pieces. The smallest piece is called a satoshi, named after its creator.

Chapter six. Your keys, your coins. So where do your bitcoins actually live? Here's something surprising. Bitcoins don't really live anywhere. They're just entries on the blockchain. What you actually own is a key. Think of it like a mailbox with a glass front. Everyone can see the mailbox. Everyone can see what's inside. And anyone can drop something in through the slot. That's your public address. But only one person has the key to open it and take things out. That's your private key. Whoever holds the private key controls the bitcoin. Period. There's no bank to call. No password reset button. No customer service. And that has led to some heartbreaking stories. In {2013|twenty thirteen}, a man in Wales named James Howells accidentally threw away an old hard drive. On it was the key to around eight thousand bitcoins. That hard drive is believed to be buried somewhere in a landfill. For years, he has tried to get permission to dig it up. And he's never succeeded. He's not alone. Experts estimate that millions of bitcoins are lost forever. Forgotten passwords. Lost hard drives. People who passed away without telling anyone their keys. Those coins still exist on the blockchain. But nobody will ever be able to use them again.

Chapter seven. The wild west years. In the early days, bitcoin was a toy for computer nerds. It was worth almost nothing. A fraction of a penny. Which is why Laszlo's pizza deal seemed perfectly reasonable at the time. But slowly, people started to notice it. Unfortunately, some of the first people to really use bitcoin weren't the best crowd. An illegal online marketplace called Silk Road used bitcoin for payments, because people thought it was anonymous. In {2013|twenty thirteen}, the FBI shut it down. And that taught the world an important lesson. Bitcoin isn't actually anonymous. Every transaction is public, forever, on the blockchain. Investigators have gotten very, very good at following the trail. Then came the first big disaster. A company called {Mt. Gox|mount gox}, based in Japan, was once the biggest bitcoin exchange in the world, handling most of the bitcoin trading on the planet. In {2014|twenty fourteen}, it suddenly collapsed. Around eight hundred and fifty thousand bitcoins were missing, mostly stolen by hackers over time. Thousands of customers lost their money. It took around ten years before many of them started getting some of it back. Many people thought that was the end of bitcoin. It wasn't.

Chapter eight. The roller coaster. If there's one thing bitcoin is famous for, it's wild price swings. In {2017|twenty seventeen}, bitcoin mania hit the mainstream. Prices shot from about a thousand dollars to nearly twenty thousand dollars in one year. People were talking about it at dinner tables everywhere. And then it crashed. Over the next year, it lost more than eighty percent of its value. Then in {2021|twenty twenty one}, it climbed again, all the way to around {sixty-nine|sixty nine} thousand dollars. Big companies like Tesla bought bitcoin. In September {2021|twenty twenty one}, El Salvador became the first country to make bitcoin official money, alongside the US dollar. Then came another crash in {2022|twenty twenty two}. And the crypto world was rocked by another scandal. A huge crypto exchange called FTX, run by a young billionaire named Sam {Bankman-Fried|Bankman Fried}, collapsed almost overnight. Billions of dollars of customer money had been misused. He was later convicted of fraud and sentenced to {twenty-five|twenty five} years in prison. Once again, people said bitcoin was dead. And once again, it came back. In January {2024|twenty twenty four}, US regulators approved bitcoin investment funds called ETFs. That meant regular investors could buy into bitcoin through their normal stock accounts, just like buying a share of a company. Huge Wall Street firms jumped in. In December {2024|twenty twenty four}, bitcoin crossed one hundred thousand dollars for the first time. In {2025|twenty twenty five}, it climbed even higher, at one point above one hundred and twenty thousand dollars. And the United States government announced it would hold a national reserve of bitcoin. The thing that started as a {nine-page|nine page} paper by an anonymous stranger was now being discussed by presidents.

Chapter nine. The case against bitcoin. Now, to be fair, not everyone is a fan. Far from it. Let's look at the biggest criticisms. First, the price swings. Bitcoin can drop thirty, fifty, even eighty percent. That makes it really hard to use as everyday money. Imagine getting paid on Monday and your paycheck being worth half as much by Friday. Second, energy. All that mining uses a massive amount of electricity. By some estimates, the bitcoin network uses about as much electricity as an entire {medium-sized|medium sized} country. Supporters argue that more and more of it comes from renewable energy, and from power that would otherwise go to waste. Critics say it's still far too much. Third, it's slow and limited. The main bitcoin network can only handle a handful of transactions per second. Visa can handle thousands. Developers have built extra systems on top of bitcoin to make it faster, but it's still a work in progress. Fourth, scams. Because bitcoin payments can't be reversed, scammers love it. Fake investment schemes. Fake celebrity giveaways. People pretending to be someone they're not. Once bitcoin is sent, there's no bank to get it back. That's why so many experts say, be extremely careful, and never trust anyone who promises guaranteed profits. And fifth, the big question. What is bitcoin actually worth? It doesn't make products. It doesn't pay a dividend. Its value depends entirely on what people believe it's worth. Some famous investors have called it worthless. Others call it the most important invention in finance since paper money.

Chapter ten. So what is bitcoin, really? So let's put it all together, simply. Bitcoin is digital money that no government or bank controls. It runs on a giant shared notebook called the blockchain, copied on thousands of computers all over the world. Miners keep the notebook honest by competing to solve puzzles, and they're rewarded with new bitcoins. There will only ever be {twenty-one|twenty one} million of them. And whoever holds the private key, controls the coins. It was born from the {2008|two thousand eight} financial crisis. It was created by someone who vanished without a trace. It survived hacks, scandals, crashes, and countless predictions of its death. And somehow, it went from paying for two pizzas, to being held by Wall Street giants and governments. Will it become the money of the future? Digital gold? Or one of the biggest bubbles in history? Honestly, nobody knows for sure. And anyone who tells you they know for certain is probably selling something. But one thing is clear. Bitcoin changed how the world thinks about money. It showed that a group of strangers, spread across the planet, could agree on a shared record of value, without needing anyone in charge. And somewhere out there, maybe, Satoshi Nakamoto is watching. With a million untouched coins. Saying nothing at all. And Laszlo? He's said he doesn't regret the pizzas. He was helping prove that bitcoin could actually be used to buy something real.
