If children can be born out of wedlock and love can happen without marriage, why did humans create the institution of marriage in the first place?
The concept of marriage is found in almost every religion across the world. Do you know why? Because marriage did not originate for the sake of love. Centuries ago, when humans lived in the wild, the biggest challenge was not romance. It was survival. Questions like: Who will take responsibility for the children? How will the family survive? And how will stability be maintained in society?
When humans lived in small groups, attraction was completely natural, and the obvious result of attraction was children. Unlike other animals, a human baby cannot start walking right after birth; it takes years to grow up. If a man and a woman did not commit to staying together, a mother alone could not protect her child from the dangers of the wild. Therefore, marriage was not a symbol of love; it was an age-old strategy for survival and economic security, ensuring society did not descend into chaos and children had a safe roof over their heads.
Today, however, there are no jungles to survive, women earn their own living, and live-in relationships exist. So why still get married? Because today, emotional, legal, and financial complexities are far greater. The purpose of marriage is not merely to celebrate love, but to transform love into responsibility.
This is why Hindu tradition never viewed marriage as a mere contract; instead, marriage was regarded as a sacred rite, a samskara. Here, marriage does not just mean living together; it signifies two people embarking together on the journey of Dharma, Artha, Kama, and Moksha. That is why in a Hindu wedding, people do not merely become husband and wife. Two families and two lineages unite.
The sad reality is that today, people have started viewing marriage merely as an event, whereas in earlier times, it was seen as a lifelong responsibility.
