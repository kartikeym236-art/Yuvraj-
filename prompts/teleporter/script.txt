To teleport, you would first have to be destroyed. And whoever arrived would have your face, your voice, and all your memories. But it might not be you. And it wouldn't be a machine failure; it's imposed by the laws of physics.
Teleportation already exists, but only with particles. In 2017, it was achieved with particles of light between the ground and a satellite at a distance of up to 1,400 kilometers. What travels is not the particle, but its information. On the other side, a second particle receives it and becomes an exact replica of the first. But physics forbids copying a particle while preserving the original. Therefore, by reading it, its original state is destroyed.
Now let's apply that rule to your body, which contains about 7,000 quadrillion atoms. A machine would have to read all of them, and that scanning would destroy you on Earth. Your information would travel to Mars at the speed of light. And there, an identical person would be assembled, atom by atom. They would open their eyes, remember stepping into the booth, and say, "I've arrived."
To determine whether that person is actually you, philosopher Derek Parfit proposed a variation in 1984. The machine makes the copy, but this time, it doesn't destroy you. You remain on Earth and watch on a screen as someone with your face starts talking on Mars. You are still here, so that person is someone else. Yet they are the exact same person who arrives when the machine does destroy you.
Parfit concluded that what matters are your memories and your character, not remaining the exact same person. Other philosophers consider it simply a way to die. And we could never test it, because the only person capable of answering is the one who arrives—and they will always say it worked.
