In March of the year 2000, the most valuable company in the world wasn't Microsoft. It wasn't a bank. It wasn't an oil giant.

It was a company called Cisco. A company that made the equipment that powered the internet. Routers. Switches. The "picks and shovels" of the internet gold rush.

Everyone agreed the internet would change the world. And they were right. It did.

But here's the twist. Within about two years, Cisco's stock lost more than eighty percent of its value. And it took decades for its stock to recover.

The internet was real. The bubble was real too.

Now fast forward to today. The most valuable company in the world became Nvidia. A company that makes the chips that power artificial intelligence. The picks and shovels of the AI gold rush.

Sound familiar?

Right now, some of the smartest investors on the planet are asking the same question. Are we living through the biggest technology boom in history? Or the biggest bubble?

Today, I'm going to explain the AI bubble debate from the very beginning. What a bubble actually is. Why so many people are worried. Why others say this time really is different. And what could happen next.

Let's go.

Chapter one. What is a bubble, anyway?

Let's start simple.

Imagine a kid at school starts selling rare trading cards. At first, a card costs one dollar. Then a rumor goes around that these cards are going to be super valuable one day. So more kids want them. The price goes up to five dollars. Then ten. Then fifty.

Now, here's the important part. Kids aren't buying the cards because they love the cards. They're buying them because they think someone else will pay even more tomorrow.

And that works... until it doesn't. One day, there's nobody left who wants to pay more. Someone tries to sell. Then another. And suddenly, everyone is trying to sell at once. The price crashes. Back to one dollar. Or less.

That's a bubble. When the price of something rises way above what it's actually worth, mostly because people expect the price to keep rising.

And here's something important. A bubble doesn't mean the thing is fake. Tulips were real flowers. Houses in 2008 were real houses. The internet in 2000 was a real revolution.

The problem isn't the idea. The problem is the price people pay for it, and how fast they expect it to pay off.

Chapter two. Lessons from history.

History is full of bubbles around exciting new technology. And they tend to follow the same pattern.

In the 1840s, Britain went crazy for railways. Trains were the most exciting technology in the world. Investors poured money into hundreds of new railway companies. Many of those lines were never built, or never made money. When the bubble burst, thousands of people lost their savings.

But Britain was left with something valuable. A national railway network that powered its economy for a century.

Then came the dot-com bubble in the late 1990s. Any company with ".com" in its name could raise millions. A website called Pets.com, which sold pet food online, became famous for its sock puppet mascot. It went public in early 2000, and shut down that same year.

Telecom companies laid down enormous amounts of fiber optic cable across the world, expecting internet traffic to explode overnight. When the bubble burst, the vast majority of that cable sat unused. People called it "dark fiber."

Between 2000 and 2002, the Nasdaq, the stock index full of tech companies, fell almost eighty percent. Trillions of dollars in value vanished.

Even Amazon's stock dropped more than ninety percent. Ninety percent!

And yet. Amazon survived. That dark fiber eventually got used, and helped power streaming, social media, and the modern internet. The technology won. Many of the early investors lost.

That's the pattern. The technology changes the world. But the people who pay too much, too early, often get crushed.

So the real question isn't, "Is AI real?" It obviously is. The question is, "Are people paying too much, too early?"

Chapter three. The AI gold rush.

Let's rewind to November thirtieth, 2022. That's when OpenAI released ChatGPT.

Within about two months, it had an estimated one hundred million users. By late 2025, OpenAI said around eight hundred million people were using it every week.

Suddenly, every tech company on Earth realized something. If AI is the future, they can't afford to be left behind.

And building AI is incredibly expensive. You need enormous buildings full of powerful computer chips, called data centers. These data centers need massive amounts of electricity, cooling, land, and networking.

So the spending began. And it kept growing.

Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and Meta started spending hundreds of billions of dollars a year on AI infrastructure. In 2025 alone, their combined spending was estimated at roughly four hundred billion dollars. And the plans for the years ahead were even bigger.

In January 2025, OpenAI and its partners announced a project called Stargate, a plan to invest up to five hundred billion dollars in AI data centers in the United States.

Meta talked about building data centers so big, one would cover an area comparable to a large part of Manhattan.

The numbers are almost impossible to imagine. This is one of the biggest building sprees in the history of business.

And the stock market went wild. Nvidia, the main company selling the chips, went from being worth a few hundred billion dollars to becoming the first company ever worth five trillion dollars, in October 2025.

By then, a small group of giant tech companies made up roughly a third of the entire value of the S&P 500, the index that tracks the five hundred biggest companies in America.

That means if those few companies stumble, the whole market feels it. Including the retirement savings of millions of regular people.

Chapter four. Why people think it's a bubble.

So let's look at the warning signs. There are five big ones.

Warning sign number one. The money going in is much bigger than the money coming out.

Companies are spending hundreds of billions of dollars on AI every year. But how much money are they actually making from AI? A lot less.

OpenAI, the most famous AI company in the world, was reported to be making around thirteen billion dollars in revenue in 2025. That's a lot of money. But it had also signed deals for computing power that added up to more than a trillion dollars over the coming years.

Imagine a lemonade stand that makes a hundred dollars a year, signing contracts to spend ten thousand dollars on lemons. It only works if the lemonade business grows incredibly fast.

Warning sign number two. Money going in circles.

This one is a little tricky, so let's keep it simple.

In 2025, Nvidia announced it would invest up to one hundred billion dollars in OpenAI. And OpenAI planned to use huge amounts of money to buy Nvidia chips.

Around the same time, OpenAI signed a giant deal with chipmaker AMD, which included an option for OpenAI to get a big chunk of AMD stock. And OpenAI also signed an enormous deal for cloud computing with Oracle, reportedly worth around three hundred billion dollars. And Oracle, in turn, buys chips from Nvidia.

So imagine three friends. Friend A gives friend B money. Friend B uses it to buy stuff from friend A. Friend A's sales go up. Everyone looks richer.

Critics call this circular financing. It doesn't mean anything illegal is happening. But it can make demand look stronger than it really is. And it means if one big player gets into trouble, it could drag the others down with it.

Warning sign number three. Borrowed money.

At first, big tech companies paid for AI with their own enormous profits. But the spending got so big that many started borrowing. Companies like Oracle and Meta raised tens of billions of dollars in debt to build data centers. Some of these deals were set up in complicated ways that kept the debt off their main books.

And remember from history. Bubbles get much more dangerous when they're built on borrowed money.

Warning sign number four. Chips get old fast.

Here's something most people don't think about. An AI chip isn't like a railroad, or a fiber optic cable, which can be useful for decades. AI chips can become outdated within just a few years, because newer, faster chips keep coming out.

So companies are spending billions on equipment that may need to be replaced again, and again. Some investors, including Michael Burry, the man famous for predicting the 2008 housing crash in the movie The Big Short, argued that some tech companies may be overstating how long these chips will stay valuable. And that could make their profits look better than they really are.

Warning sign number five. Even insiders are nervous.

In August 2025, OpenAI's own CEO, Sam Altman, said he thought investors as a whole were overexcited about AI. When asked if we're in a bubble, his answer was, essentially, yes, in some ways.

That same month, a report from researchers at MIT made headlines claiming that around ninety-five percent of companies' generative AI pilot projects weren't producing measurable financial returns. The study had its critics, but it hit a nerve.

And around the world, central banks and financial watchdogs, including the Bank of England and the International Monetary Fund, started warning that AI stock prices looked stretched, and that a sharp correction was a real risk.

When the people building the boom start warning about the boom, people pay attention.

Chapter five. Why others say it's NOT a bubble.

Now, to be fair, there's another side to this story. And it's a strong one.

Argument number one. These companies make real money.

In the dot-com bubble, many companies had almost no revenue. Pets.com was losing money on almost every bag of pet food it sold.

Today's AI leaders are different. Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and Meta are some of the most profitable companies in human history. And Nvidia is making tens of billions of dollars in profit every few months.

Argument number two. The prices aren't as crazy as 2000.

Investors often look at something called the price-to-earnings ratio. Simply put, how expensive a stock is, compared to the profit the company makes.

At the peak of the dot-com bubble, Cisco's stock was priced at well over one hundred times its yearly earnings. Today's big AI stocks are expensive. But by that measure, most of them aren't nearly as extreme as Cisco was back then.

Argument number three. People are actually using it.

Hundreds of millions of people use AI chatbots every week. Students. Programmers. Doctors. Businesses. AI is helping write code, answer customer questions, discover new medicines, and much more.

And there's a real shortage. For years, companies couldn't get enough AI chips, no matter how much they were willing to pay.

Argument number four. The leaders say demand is real.

When asked about the AI bubble, Nvidia's CEO Jensen Huang pushed back. He argued that the world is going through a massive shift in how computing works, and that demand for AI keeps growing.

And supporters argue, even if some companies fail, AI will still transform the economy. Just like the internet did after the dot-com crash.

Chapter six. So... what could actually happen?

So, is it a bubble? Let's look at the possible endings.

Scenario one. The soft landing.

AI keeps getting better, and businesses figure out how to make serious money from it. Revenue grows fast enough to justify all the spending. Stock prices might wobble, but the boom continues. Everyone who said "bubble" looks silly.

Scenario two. The air slowly leaks out.

AI is useful, but the profits grow slower than expected. Companies quietly cut back on spending. Stocks don't crash all at once, but they go sideways or slowly drift down for years. Disappointing, but not a disaster.

Scenario three. The pop.

Something triggers panic. Maybe a big AI company runs out of money. Maybe a cheaper AI breakthrough means companies need far fewer chips. We actually got a small preview of this in January 2025, when a Chinese company called DeepSeek released an AI model it said was trained for a fraction of the usual cost. In a single day, Nvidia lost nearly six hundred billion dollars in stock market value. The biggest one-day loss for any company in history.

In a real pop, AI stocks could fall dramatically. And because those few giant companies make up such a big part of the stock market, it could affect retirement accounts, jobs, and the wider economy.

But here's the thing. Even in scenario three, history suggests AI wouldn't disappear. Just like the internet didn't disappear in 2001. The data centers would still exist. The technology would keep improving. It would just be cheaper, and owned by the survivors.

Chapter seven. The bottom line.

So let's put it all together.

A bubble happens when prices rise far beyond what something is really worth, because everyone expects them to keep rising.

AI is clearly a real and powerful technology. That part isn't in doubt.

But companies are spending hundreds of billions of dollars, much faster than AI is making money. Some of that money is going in circles between the same companies. More of it is being borrowed. And the chips being bought can get outdated quickly.

On the other hand, the biggest players are hugely profitable, AI is being used by hundreds of millions of people, and today's prices aren't as extreme as the peak of the dot-com bubble.

So is it a bubble? The honest answer is, parts of it probably are. Some companies will likely turn out to be the next Amazon. And some will likely turn out to be the next Pets.com.

The hard part is, nobody knows which ones yet.

As the famous investor Warren Buffett once put it, you only find out who's swimming naked when the tide goes out.
